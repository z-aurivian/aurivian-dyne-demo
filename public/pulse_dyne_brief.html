Intelligence Brief ● Critical FORCE™ Platform June 11, 2026 · 6:00 AM ET

The ACHIEVE readout window is 6 months away. The DM1 narrative is being written without Dyne's voice in it.

DYNE-101's Phase 3 ACHIEVE trial readout is the most important catalytic event in DM1 in a decade. But the conversation about DM1 treatment expectations is being shaped right now — in patient forums, neurologist roundtables, and WMS abstract submissions — before the data exists. The field is not waiting. Here is what the four communities are saying.

● Central Tension  ·  4 Communities Converging

The central tension: Dyne's platform differentiation (skeletal muscle tissue selectivity) is a scientific argument that has not yet become a clinical narrative. Meanwhile, gene therapy is capturing 'curative' framing across neuromuscular diseases — including DMD and DM1 — setting an expectation bar that RNA-targeting approaches will need to address in the context of their mechanism, not compete against.

01 Providers ↑ Accelerating Signal

Neuromuscular specialists are building DM1 expectations around ACHIEVE. The data package narrative needs to start before the data.

WMS 2026 pre-congress abstract submission analysis shows DM1 is the most discussed rare neuromuscular indication by abstract volume, up 34% vs WMS 2025. Three academic medical centers with high DM1 patient volumes have begun actively counseling patients on the ACHIEVE trial timeline — creating treatment expectation conversations that will precede the readout by 3–6 months. The specialists leading these conversations have not received pre-readout educational materials from Dyne.

The opportunity is the positioning window. When a neurologist counsels a DM1 patient about ACHIEVE, they are simultaneously forming their own opinion about FORCE™ mechanism plausibility. Three conversations are happening in that moment: What does the data need to show? What does 'meaningful' look like for this patient? And is RNA-targeting a mechanism worth believing in? All three are shaped by information that exists now — before the readout.

"My DM1 patients ask me every visit whether ACHIEVE has any news. I tell them what I know, which isn't much. I'd rather be telling them something clinical."

Neuromuscular specialist · MDA symposium informal commentary · May 2026

02 Patients ● New Narrative

DM1 patient communities are tracking ACHIEVE with clinical sophistication. They are forming pass/fail criteria without guidance.

MyDM1 Foundation forum and DM1 patient group monitoring shows an acceleration in ACHIEVE-related discussion over the past 8 weeks — 89 posts referencing the trial directly, up from 12 in the prior 8 weeks. Patient posts show a high level of clinical literacy: discussions reference specific endpoints (MIRS score, hand grip strength, gait), trial phase and design, and comparisons to prior failed trials. This community is forming independent expectations.

The strategic risk is expectation misalignment. Patient communities are setting a pass/fail bar that may not match the trial's pre-specified primary endpoint. Three recurring themes in patient posts: "will this stop disease progression?" (not the primary endpoint), "will it work in all DM1 subtypes?" (trial enrolled classic DM1 primarily), and "how fast will it become available?" (approval pathway questions). None of these are being guided by current Dyne medical communications.

"I've been in three failed trials. I'm not getting my hopes up about ACHIEVE until someone explains to me why FORCE is different from what we've seen before."

DM1 patient · MyDM1 Foundation forum · June 2026

03 Payers ↑ Accelerating Signal

Ultra-rare disease payers are building DMD/DM1 pricing frameworks now. Dyne is not in that conversation.

Post-Elevidys approval (Sarepta's DMD gene therapy, $3.2M), major PBMs and state Medicaid programs began building ultra-rare neuromuscular disease coverage frameworks. These frameworks are designed to handle multiple approvals across DMD, DM1, and Pompe — and they are being constructed with gene therapy economics as the reference point. RNA-targeting therapies like DYNE-251 and DYNE-101 will be evaluated against a framework designed for a different mechanism and a different cost structure.

The access challenge is being architected without Dyne input. ICER and pharmacy benefit frameworks for rare neuromuscular disease are referencing Elevidys ($3.2M), Zolgensma ($2.1M SMA), and Skyclarys (FA, $371K/year) as pricing anchors. An RNA-targeting therapy entering this landscape with a lower (but still premium) price point will be evaluated against gene therapy cost-effectiveness assumptions — not against its own mechanism-appropriate framework. The health economics argument for FORCE™ tissue selectivity has not been made.

"We've built our rare neuromuscular framework around gene therapy economics. An RNA therapy would need to explain to me why its cost-effectiveness model is different from the gene therapy reference point before I could evaluate it on its own terms."

PBM rare disease pharmacy director · AMCP 2026

04 Competitive ↑ Accelerating Signal

Gene therapy is capturing the 'curative' frame. RNA-targeting is being positioned as the second-best option before the data exists.

Sarepta's Elevidys approval (DMD, micro-dystrophin gene therapy) has reshaped the narrative architecture of the entire neuromuscular disease space. At every major conference in the past 12 months, gene therapy is presented as the eventual destination — and RNA-targeting approaches are implicitly framed as bridges or alternatives for patients who can't access or tolerate gene therapy. This positioning is not driven by clinical evidence comparing the approaches. It is driven by narrative sequencing.

The FORCE™ differentiation argument — skeletal muscle tissue selectivity, RNA targeting of the disease mechanism rather than compensation, potentially applicable across multiple genetic diseases — has not been presented as a competitive frame against gene therapy. It has been presented as a mechanism description. The distinction matters: a mechanism description asks "how does this work?" A competitive frame asks "why is this the right approach for this patient?" Dyne is answering the first question. The field is asking the second.

"When I explain DM1 to a family, I now have to explain gene therapy first and then explain why we're not using it. That's the order of the conversation. It wasn't that way three years ago."

Neuromuscular specialist · WMS 2025 abstract commentary

Intelligence Brief Payer · Access Structural Gap June 6, 2026 · 7:30 AM ET

Ultra-rare payer frameworks are being built now — with gene therapy economics as the reference point. FORCE™ tissue selectivity has no cost-effectiveness argument in those frameworks.

Post-Elevidys approval, major PBMs and state Medicaid programs began constructing ultra-rare neuromuscular disease coverage frameworks. These frameworks use one-time gene therapy economics as the baseline. RNA-targeting therapies — which require chronic dosing but offer a fundamentally different risk profile and cost trajectory — are being evaluated against gene therapy comparators, not their own mechanism-appropriate health economics.

The access picture: ICER's upcoming rare neuromuscular disease review will establish cost-effectiveness benchmarks. Without a FORCE™-specific HEOR submission that makes the tissue-selectivity, chronic-vs-one-time, and mechanism-appropriateness arguments, Dyne's therapies will be evaluated against gene therapy utility models — which are structurally disadvantageous for RNA-targeting approaches. The ICER comment period window closes before ACHIEVE reads out.

01 Payers ↑ Structural Hardening

The HEOR argument for FORCE™ tissue selectivity needs to reach ICER before the framework is set. Post-ACHIEVE is too late.

ICER's methodology for ultra-rare neurological disease values treatments against QALYs in a population that is small, severely affected, and has no standard of care. This methodology is theoretically favorable for FORCE™ — but only if the tissue-selectivity argument is made explicitly. The comparative efficacy section of ICER's rare neuromuscular framework currently references only approved gene therapies and IVIg as contextual comparators. RNA-targeting therapies are not mentioned.
